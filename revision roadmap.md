Devil's Advocate Review — Dr. Erik Sandvik

Methodologist & Skeptical Reviewer

Strongest Counter-Argument

The paper's central intellectual contribution — the "diagnostic triad" of negative colonoscopy, normal tumor markers, and non-enhancing mass on CECT — is presented as a clinical framework pointing toward pelvic epidermoid cyst. This framing is logically flawed.

All three elements of the triad are exclusionary negatives, not positive diagnostic features of epidermoid cysts. Negative colonoscopy confirms extrinsic origin but does not differentiate epidermoid cyst from retroperitoneal sarcoma, Müllerian cyst, lymphangioma, mature cystic teratoma, presacral hamartoma, or anterior sacral meningocele. Normal tumor markers exclude common carcinomas but not the full differential. Non-enhancement excludes hypervascular tumors but not other cystic lesions (mesenteric cyst, lymphangioma, mucinous cystadenoma). The triad narrows the differential to "benign, non-enhancing, extrinsic pelvic cystic mass" — a category with at least 8–10 entities. What ultimately diagnosed this case as an epidermoid cyst was histopathology, not the triad.

The current framing implies the triad has diagnostic specificity for epidermoid cyst that it does not possess. Accepted as written, this paper would mislead clinicians into thinking the triad identifies the diagnosis rather than simply directing toward surgical excision for histopathological confirmation.

Ignored Alternative Explanations

- L2 compression fracture: never investigated or dismissed. In an older male with a 10 cm pelvic mass, occult malignancy with spinal metastasis should at minimum be considered and excluded in one sentence.
- Hematuria: the cause is never identified or resolved in the narrative.

Missing Stakeholder Perspectives

- Pathologist perspective absent: Were IHC stains (p63, CK5/6 for squamous lineage; calretinin/WT-1 to exclude mesothelioma) performed? The paper mentions mesothelioma is excluded by squamous lining, but this should be confirmed by IHC, not gross morphology alone.
- Patient's post-operative symptom resolution: tenesmus, bowel habit changes, hematuria — all presenting symptoms — are never confirmed resolved at discharge or follow-up.

"So What?" Test

The paper's answer — "this rare cyst mimics malignancy; these three clues help" — is a valid contribution for general surgeons in settings without MRI access. In well-resourced centres, MRI pelvis would typically resolve the diagnostic question pre-operatively, rendering the triad less operationally relevant. This applicability boundary should be stated explicitly.

Editorial Decision Letter

Decision: Major Revision

Dear Authors,

Thank you for submitting your case report on a large epidermoid cyst of the pelvic floor. The reviewers agree that this is a rare and clinically instructive case. However, the manuscript requires substantial revision before it can be considered for publication.

Two issues are blocking and must be resolved before re-review:

1. Figure 1 (H&E histopathology) is absent. This is the definitive evidence supporting your diagnosis. A case report on pathological diagnosis cannot proceed to editorial review without it.
2. The "diagnostic triad" framing must be recalibrated. As currently written, the triad is presented as pointing specifically toward epidermoid cyst, but all three elements are exclusionary negatives that point toward a benign non-enhancing extrinsic cystic pelvic mass — a category containing multiple entities. The diagnosis of epidermoid cyst was ultimately established by histopathology. The triad's contribution is directing toward surgical excision for histological confirmation, not toward a specific tissue diagnosis. This overclaim, if uncorrected, constitutes a logical error that experienced reviewers will reject.
